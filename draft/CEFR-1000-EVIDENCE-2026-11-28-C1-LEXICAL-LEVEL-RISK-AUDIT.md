### 1. coalesce — CEFR1000-C1-0975

- **Selected sense:** intransitive verb, two or more things come or grow together to form one thing or system.
- **Cambridge sense evidence:** Cambridge Advanced Learner's Dictionary gives this exact meaning and marks the register formal: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/coalesce
- **CEFR evidence:** no CEFR label was visible on the inspected Cambridge entry. The page supports meaning and register, not C1 placement.
- **British pronunciation reference:** /kəʊ.əˈles/.
- **Myanmar:** `ပေါင်းစည်းကာ တစ်ခုတည်းသော အဖွဲ့ သို့မဟုတ် အစုအဖြစ် ဖြစ်လာသည်` is a useful starting gloss; check whether the chosen example is intransitive and whether the Myanmar sentence expresses gradual joining rather than a deliberate merger.
- **Existing example:** “Several small community groups began to coalesce around the proposal.” Grammatically plausible; `coalesce around` describes groups gathering around a shared idea, not necessarily literally becoming one organisation.
- **Original alternative:** “The separate committees eventually coalesced into one national organisation.”
- **Disposition:** exact meaning is supported, but C1 remains unsubstantiated by the inspected source. Keep on hold pending an explicit CEFR source or a documented independent level decision.

### 2. conjecture — CEFR1000-C1-0976

- **Selected sense:** formal noun, a guess or explanation formed without proof or sufficient evidence.
- **Cambridge sense evidence:** Cambridge Learner's Dictionary describes guesses about something without real evidence; Cambridge English Dictionary describes a guess based on appearances rather than proof: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/learner-english/conjecture and https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/conjecture
- **CEFR evidence:** no CEFR label was visible on the inspected entries. Formal register and dictionary inclusion do not establish C1.
- **British pronunciation reference:** /kənˈdʒek.tʃə/.
- **Definition refinement for reviewer consideration:** “A guess or explanation formed without enough evidence.” This is closer to the Cambridge sense than “an opinion or explanation based on incomplete evidence”, which can imply that some supporting evidence exists.
- **Myanmar:** `အထောက်အထား မလုံလောက်ဘဲ ခန့်မှန်းယူဆထားသော အမြင် သို့မဟုတ် ရှင်းလင်းချက်`.
- **Existing example:** “Without test results, the cause of the failure remains conjecture.” Grammatically sound; `remains conjecture` uses the noun uncountably.
- **Original alternative:** “Until the records are recovered, any account of the missing shipment is conjecture.”
- **Disposition:** exact sense is supported; C1 is not established by the inspected source. Keep on hold.

### 3. conscientious — CEFR1000-C1-0977

- **Selected sense:** adjective describing someone who puts substantial effort into work and does it carefully.
- **Cambridge CEFR evidence:** Cambridge labels the selected work-effort sense **C1**: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/conscientious
- **British pronunciation reference:** /ˌkɒn.ʃiˈen.ʃəs/.
- **Level finding:** the C1 label matches the selected work-related adjective sense. Cambridge's American/Academic entry also describes a moral-responsibility nuance; do not merge that nuance indiscriminately with the selected UK C1 wording.
- **Myanmar:** `တာဝန်ကို အားစိုက်၍ အသေးစိတ် ဂရုတစိုက် ဆောင်ရွက်တတ်သော` is more specific than a generic `တာဝန်သိသော` alone.
- **Existing example:** “A conscientious inspector records even small defects in the report.” Grammatically correct and aligned with careful work.
- **Original alternative:** “A conscientious auditor checks every figure before signing the report.”
- **Disposition:** exact-sense/level match in author research; independent bilingual, example and CEFR signoff is still required.

### 4. culpability — CEFR1000-C1-0978

- **Selected sense:** uncountable noun, the fact of deserving blame or being considered responsible for something bad.
- **Cambridge sense evidence:** Cambridge Advanced Learner's Dictionary defines the noun in terms of deserving blame or responsibility for something bad: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/culpability
- **CEFR evidence:** no CEFR label was visible on the inspected Cambridge entry. The legal/formal flavour does not itself prove C1.
- **British pronunciation reference:** /ˌkʌl.pəˈbɪl.ə.ti/.
- **Definition refinement:** “The fact of deserving blame or being responsible for something wrong.” This is closer to the source than the broader “responsibility for a mistake or harmful action”.
- **Myanmar:** `အမှား သို့မဟုတ် မကောင်းသောလုပ်ရပ်အတွက် အပြစ်တင်ခံထိုက်သည့် တာဝန်ရှိမှု`.
- **Original example:** “The inquiry examined the contractor's culpability in the safety breach.”
- **Grammar note:** normally uncountable in this selected use; common patterns include `accept culpability`, `deny culpability`, and `culpability for`.
- **Disposition:** meaning is supported, C1 is not. Keep on hold.

### 5. disparage — CEFR1000-C1-0979

- **Selected sense:** transitive verb, to criticise someone or something in a way that shows little respect or value.
- **Cambridge sense evidence:** Cambridge Advanced Learner's Dictionary gives this exact meaning and the transitive pattern: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/disparage
- **CEFR evidence:** no CEFR label was visible on the inspected entry. The formal/negative tone is not a CEFR classification.
- **British pronunciation reference:** /dɪˈspær.ɪdʒ/.
- **Myanmar:** `တန်ဖိုးမထားသကဲ့သို့ နှိမ့်ချပြောဆိုသည်` captures the disrespectful criticism better than a neutral `ဝေဖန်သည်`.
- **Existing example:** “A professional manager should not disparage colleagues in meetings.” Grammatically correct; the object `colleagues` is explicit.
- **Original alternative:** “A good leader corrects mistakes without disparaging the people who made them.”
- **Disposition:** exact sense is supported; C1 remains unsubstantiated. Keep on hold.

### 6. disproportionate — CEFR1000-C1-0980

- **Selected sense:** adjective, too large or too small in comparison with something else.
- **Cambridge sense evidence:** Cambridge gives this comparative-proportion meaning and the pattern `disproportionate to`: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/disproportionate
- **CEFR evidence:** no CEFR label was visible on the inspected entry. Do not infer C1 from length or formality.
- **British pronunciation reference:** /ˌdɪs.prəˈpɔː.ʃə.nət/.
- **Myanmar:** `နှိုင်းယှဉ်ရမည့်အရာနှင့် အချိုးအစား မမျှသော` is more precise than a generic `မမျှတသော`, which may suggest unfairness rather than proportion.
- **Original example:** “The penalty was disproportionate to the seriousness of the offence.”
- **Grammar note:** the `disproportionate to` complement is a useful collocation to teach; British spelling `offence` is intentional.
- **Disposition:** meaning is supported; C1 remains unsubstantiated. Keep on hold.

- **Level finding:** the C1 signal is explicit on the Cambridge translated dictionary page; the English page corroborates the meaning. An independent reviewer should confirm that the badge applies to this selected headword sense and record the page/observation directly.
- **Myanmar:** `အယူအဆ၊ ပြဿနာ သို့မဟုတ် လုပ်ထုံးလုပ်နည်းတစ်ခုကို အမြစ်တွယ်ခိုင်မာစေသည်`.
- **Existing example:** “Repeated exceptions can entrench unfair habits in an organisation.” Grammatically correct; `can entrench` takes a direct object.

- **Disposition:** promising C1 exact-sense match in author research, but still not independent approval.
